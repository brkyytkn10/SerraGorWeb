Vitamin D Before Winter: When and How Much?

Category: Functional Medicine Author: Dyt. Serra Gör Date: October 10, 2026

The days are getting shorter, and the sun's angle is dropping. In Turkey, especially between October and March, almost none of the UVB rays our skin needs to produce vitamin D actually reach us. Every autumn, I see the same pattern in my clients' blood tests: vitamin D levels that stayed normal all summer drop significantly by the middle of winter. But this decline is entirely possible to catch in advance and prevent.

Why Is Vitamin D So Important?

Vitamin D isn't just a "bone vitamin." It has a direct effect on the immune system, muscle function, and even mood:

  • Immunity: Plays a role in regulating immune cells; deficiency is linked to more frequent infections.
  • Bone and muscle health: Required for calcium absorption in the gut.
  • Mood: Because of its link to serotonin production, it may play a role in the low energy and low mood many people feel in winter.
  • Hormonal balance: A parameter we frequently assess alongside thyroid function and women's health concerns.

Who's at Risk?

Despite Turkey being a sunny country, vitamin D deficiency is quite common. The risk is especially higher in:

  • People who spend most of the day indoors (office, school)
  • People who wear modest clothing or cover most of their skin
  • Adults over 65 (the skin's vitamin D production declines with age)
  • Overweight/obese individuals (vitamin D gets stored in fat tissue and reaches the blood less)
  • People with darker skin tones (melanin reduces UVB absorption)
"It's far more effective to start vitamin D support in autumn, before your levels drop, than in the middle of winter once symptoms have already started."

When Should You Get Tested?

The ideal timing is September-October, while the effect of summer sun is still present in your body. A "25-OH vitamin D" blood test done during this window shows what level you'll be entering winter with, and lets you start supplementation early if needed. Testing in the middle of winter (January-February) is of course still useful, but by that point a deficiency has often already set in.

Supporting It Through Food

The amount of vitamin D you get from food alone usually isn't enough on its own, but it does strengthen your overall support:

  • Oily fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines)
  • Egg yolk
  • Vitamin D-fortified milk and dairy products
  • Sun-exposed mushrooms

Important note: Vitamin D is fat-soluble and can build up in the body. Based on your blood test results, don't take high-dose supplements on your own without determining the right dose together with your doctor and dietitian — too much can be just as problematic as too little.
